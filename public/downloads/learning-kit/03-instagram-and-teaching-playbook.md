# Turn your AI experiments into lessons and Instagram videos

**Teaching and content guide · 14 November 2026**

Your advantage as a teacher should be a tested process: you can explain the idea, run the task, inspect the result, and help someone else reproduce it. This playbook turns the [main guide](01-ai-from-zero-to-advanced.md) and [30 labs](02-practice-labs.md) into a learning series.

The schedule, episode lengths, and production choices below are editorial recommendations. They are not claims about an Instagram algorithm or a guarantee of growth.

## 1. A six-week progression

Use five focused sessions per week if your schedule allows. A session might be 45–90 minutes, with specialist labs taking longer. Slow down when the check fails. Do not publish five rushed lessons merely to match the table.

| Week | Your work | Evidence to keep | Readiness check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1: foundations | Main guide sections 1–5; Labs 1–4 | Glossary, task brief, fact sheet, conflict table | Explain model/app/agent and resolve the source conflict without help |
| 2: reliable information | Labs 5–10 | Calculations, labels, model comparison, verified announcement, source notebook | Reproduce known answers, identify missing evidence, and describe comparison limits |
| 3: writing and building | Labs 11–15 | Script variants, review notes, bug fix, small application | Show a meaningful check and explain one failure a pretty output could hide |
| 4: systems | Labs 16–22; selected agents deep dive | Agent trace, handoff, routing/graph diagrams, skill review | Explain why you chose one prompt, a workflow, or an agent for a task |
| 5: media | Labs 23–28 | Original briefs, all attempts, selected outputs, preservation checks | Demonstrate generation and editing as separate skills; preserve critical text/numbers |
| 6: teaching and integration | Labs 29–30; revise weaker experiments | Normal/failure workflow traces, lesson pack, learner feedback | A learner completes an unseen comparable task and explains the check |

Your first course can stop before advanced frameworks. If learners cannot distinguish approved facts from a proposal, adding orchestration will multiply confusion. Introduce technical depth when the basic workflow is understandable.

## 2. The preparation loop for every episode

1. **Choose one learning outcome.** “Recognize an app feature versus a model capability” is teachable in one short episode. “Everything about AI” needs a series.
2. **Verify the factual claims.** For product news, open the official source and record date, access, and status.
3. **Run the relevant task.** Save the prompt, input, output, model/app, attempts, and corrections.
4. **Inspect the result.** Use a source, known answer, or meaningful acceptance check.
5. **Choose the honest story.** A failure, success, or mixed result can all teach something.
6. **Write the script around the observation.** Do not claim a hands-on result before the test.
7. **Give viewers a small exercise.** They should leave with something they can do.
8. **Check the lesson after a beginner tries it.** Their confusion is evidence for the next revision.

Keep two visible labels in your planning notes: **researched news** and **tested tutorial**. A news explanation does not require pretending you have used a restricted model. A tutorial should be based on an operation you actually completed.

## 4. A repeatable episode structure

For a suggested 45–75-second educational clip:

- **Opening:** Name a concrete problem or show a result worth inspecting.
- **Setup:** Show the task, input, and relevant tool/model.
- **Demonstration:** Show the operation and the observed output.
- **Check:** Explain one verification step or limitation.
- **Practice:** Give the viewer one small action to try.

This is a writing structure, not a timing rule. A complicated experiment may need a longer video or multiple episodes. Do not remove the check merely to make the claim sound simpler.

Make text large enough to read on a phone. Highlight the relevant line rather than displaying a full dense document. Keep captions consistent with the spoken claim. If generation or waiting time is cut, label the cut or state the actual elapsed time when it affects the comparison.

## 5. Three script drafts to adapt after testing

### Draft A: Model, app, and agent

**Narration:**

“When someone says ‘this AI is better,’ ask what they tested. The model is the system generating responses. The app adds features such as files, search, or voice. An agent system can choose actions with tools and use feedback to continue a task. Here is one example: preparing a lesson from documents. A model can draft the lesson. A search tool can find evidence. An agent can decide to inspect a source, revise the draft, and check the result. Before comparing outputs, write down the model, app, and tools. Otherwise you may be comparing different setups without realizing it.”

**Capture:** Your Lab 1 map and an actual interface showing available tools. Use a drawn agent loop if you have not run an agent; label it as an illustration.

**Learner action:** Identify the app and tools in one AI task they use.

### Draft B: The conflicting launch documents

**Narration:**

“These fictional documents show two launch dates. The first is a proposal. The second is an approved decision that replaces it. The newest note tells us to use that approved decision. Our answer should give 27 November and 59 US dollars, with the approved source. The refund period is not established. Now look at the AI's actual answer: [insert your observed result]. The skill here is choosing the right authority and checking the evidence. A fluent answer that invents the missing policy would fail this task.”

**Capture:** D01, D02, D03; your real prompt/output; answer-key comparison. Replace the bracketed sentence after running the lab. Do not create a fake AI failure for the story.

**Learner action:** Find the authoritative source and one unanswered question in a document set.

### Draft C: The contact bug

**Narration:**

“This small program returns four contacts. The corrected program also returns four. So did we fix anything? Yes: the identities matter. The broken version keeps a duplicate Ada and drops Cora because it mishandles spaces and blank email addresses. Here is the actual result before and after the fix. If we had checked only the count, we would have missed the bug. When AI writes code, define the behavior you need and test the records or actions themselves. A passing check is only useful if it checks the right thing.”

**Capture:** Run Lab 13 first. Show the original and corrected output identities, the specification, and the real check output. The supplied baseline behavior was independently verified; your generated fix still needs its own test.

**Learner action:** Propose one additional test case before seeing the solution.

## 6. A learner workshop you can repeat

A suggested 60-minute small-group lesson:

| Time | Activity | Your role |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 min | Ask learners to predict an outcome | Reveal their assumptions without correcting everything immediately |
| 5–15 min | Explain one idea with a shared example | Use plain language and a visual |
| 15–25 min | Demonstrate the task and its check | Show the setup, output, and evidence |
| 25–40 min | Learners attempt a comparable task | Observe; avoid taking over the keyboard |
| 40–50 min | Compare results and investigate failures | Separate conceptual confusion from tool/access problems |
| 50–60 min | Teach-back and next exercise | Ask each learner to explain the check and one limitation |

For assessment, award 0–2 each for understanding the task, providing context, producing a usable result, verifying it, and explaining limitations. Keep essential requirements separate from the score. A learner who gets a different output may still demonstrate strong reasoning by finding and correcting its defect.

Use fictional data initially. Introduce learners' real tasks later, after agreeing what information they should share and what actions the tool is permitted to take. This keeps the early lesson focused on the method.

## 7. Your evidence standards as a creator

Before presenting an episode as instruction:

- The main factual claim has a source or your own recorded experiment.
- The exact app/model and relevant date are recorded; use “not shown” when unavailable.
- The captured output is real and corresponds to the visible input.
- Retries, manual editing, and important delays are disclosed when they affect the lesson.
- Any comparison uses comparable tasks and states its sample size.
- The conclusion is narrower than or equal to the evidence.
- The viewer receives a reproducible exercise or a clearly labeled news takeaway.

Avoid claiming “best model,” “fully automatic,” “no coding,” “free,” or “works every time” unless your evidence actually supports the specific wording. “I built this flow with these tools, then corrected these two issues” is both accurate and useful.

For synthetic media, follow the platform's current disclosure controls. Meta's 2026 policy explanation says it requires disclosure for organic photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered and describes AI information labels. Check the current posting interface and policy when publishing; this is not a blanket statement that every AI-assisted text needs the same label. If you discover a mistake after posting, correct the claim and explain what changed. Keep the source and experiment record so the correction is easy to trace.

## 8. How to stay current without chasing everything

Spend one short session a week reviewing official release notes for the tools in your curriculum. Keep this registry:

| Product/version | What changed | Announcement date | Usable access/status | Primary source | My test/result | Lesson affected | Next review |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | | |

Prioritize updates that change what learners can do: capability, access, retirement, price, tool support, or a known failure. Save other news for a roundup. Do not rebuild your course around every announcement.

A useful weekly publishing rhythm is one tested method, one explanation of a recurring mistake, and one carefully sourced update when the news merits it. This is a suggested workload; begin with fewer episodes if testing and teaching take more time.

## 9. What to measure

For learning, track completed experiments, essential checks passed, errors detected independently, and the ability to repeat a task on an unseen example. For teaching, track learner completion and where they need intervention. For content, use whatever engagement and retention information the platform actually provides, but do not treat views as proof that a lesson was accurate or understood.

After each week, keep the lessons that learners can apply, revise the confusing ones, and remove claims you cannot support. This turns your experience into a curriculum that can improve as the tools change.
